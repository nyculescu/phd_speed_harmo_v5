# Review: Cathy Wu, "Learning and Optimization for Mixed Autonomy Systems - A Mobility Context" (UC Berkeley EECS-2018-132, 2018)

Source file: `text/2018_Wu_Berkeley_PhD.txt`. Page convention: `[p. N]` is the `=== [page N] ===` marker in the text file (PDF page). The printed page number is N minus 10 (file page 46 prints "36").

## 0. Scope: what in this thesis is traffic control

Only two chapters are freeway or traffic control experiments.
- Ch. 4 "Emergent behaviors in mixed autonomy traffic" [p. 45-65].
- Ch. 6 "Flow: a library for RL and microsimulation", mainly the ring-road benchmark in 6.6 [p. 91-119].

Other chapters are not traffic-control experiments.
- Ch. 5 is generic policy-gradient variance reduction. The text checked in this review (summary p. 66, intro p. 67-68) names only MuJoCo-style, hand-manipulation and synthetic tasks. Its traffic link is only the motivation "mixed autonomy traffic as introduced in Chapter 4" [p. 66].
- Ch. 7 (Cellpath, route-flow estimation), Ch. 8-10 (ridesharing, carpool, clustering) are state estimation and ridesharing, not control.
- Ramp metering and variable speed limits are named once, as forms of automation outside the thesis scope: "ramp meters, variable speed limits and other electronic roadway signage" [p. 15]. Neither is studied.

**The thesis contains no freeway bottleneck, no merge bottleneck with inflow demand, no ramp meter, no VSL, no capacity drop, and no incidents.** Every control experiment uses a closed ring or figure-eight. Searches for "capacity drop", "ramp meter" and "bottleneck" found only the passing mentions above and, for ramp metering, the related-work citation of Belletti et al. (2017a) [p. 63, p. 111].

## Chapter 4: Emergent behaviors in mixed autonomy traffic (RL with a few AVs on closed networks)

### 1. Problem and network
- **Objective.** "How can a set of automated vehicles optimize a traffic system in the presence of both automated and human-driven vehicles?" The objective is system-level velocity [p. 51].
- **Networks.** Three, "to isolate" shockwaves, lane changes and intersection queuing [p. 54]:
  - Single-lane ring. 22 vehicles on a 230 m ring (after Sugiyama et al. 2008) [p. 55].
  - Multi-lane ring. 2 lanes, 44 vehicles, circumference 230 m, six AVs [p. 58].
  - Figure-eight. Ring radius 30 m, total length 402 m, 14 vehicles. The intersection is uncontrolled and uses SUMO's right-of-way model [p. 55-56].
- **Demand.** None. These are closed networks with a fixed vehicle count. Open inflow, on-ramps and off-ramps are not modelled in Ch. 4.
- **Incidents.** None in Ch. 4. The only disturbance is IDM acceleration noise. (Ch. 6 notes that Flow can inject random braking perturbations as a feature [p. 103], but the case study is not stated to use them.)
- **Penetration.** One AV among 22 vehicles. Strings of 3-11 AVs among 22 vehicles. 100 % AVs [p. 56].
- **Length of runs.** The Ch. 4 horizon is not stated.

### 2. Simulator and models
- **Simulator.** SUMO, "all other system dynamics are represented within SUMO", including "calibrated lateral (lane-changing) dynamics for human-driven vehicles, right-of-way" [p. 52]. Flow is the library [p. 65].
- **Car following.** IDM for human drivers, "calibrated to accurately model realistic driver behavior on freeways (Treiber et al., 2000)" [p. 52]. Table 1 gives "typical values": v0 = 54 km/h, T = 1.0 s, s0 = 2 m, delta = 4, a = 1.0 m/s^2, b = 1.5 m/s^2 [p. 50]. The text does not say these are the parameters actually used. For Ch. 6 it says only "parameters specified by (Treiber and Kesting, 2013)" [p. 104].
- **Noise.** "Gaussian acceleration noise of N(0, 0.2), calibrated to match measures of stochasticity presented in (Treiber and Kesting, 2017)" [p. 52].
- **Lane change.** SUMO's default lane-change model. The AVs' lane change is an action with a cooldown [p. 52].
- **Capacity drop.** **Not modelled and not mentioned.** The thesis does not state capacity or flow numbers anywhere in the control chapters. The performance metric is mean speed on a ring.
- **Calibration or validation against field data.** Not stated beyond citing the Treiber IDM values and the Sugiyama ring setup. No loop-detector or trajectory fit is reported. The ring is compared qualitatively to the Sugiyama field experiment (stop-and-go waves) [p. 55].
- **Timestep.** Not stated for Ch. 4. Ch. 6 says SUMO car-following models are "calibrated for a simulation timestep of 1.0 seconds" and that Flow extends SUMO to "10-100ms timesteps" [p. 112]. The step actually used in the experiments is not stated.

### 3. RL formulation
- **Algorithm.** Model-free policy gradient: TRPO with linear feature baselines (Duan et al. 2016), discount 0.999, step size 0.01 [p. 65].
- **Policy.** Diagonal Gaussian MLP, hidden layers (100, 50, 25), tanh [p. 65].
- **Setting.** "Fully observed cooperative RL problem... centralized training regime and centralized execution" [p. 51]. This is a single central agent that outputs one action for all AVs.
- **State.** The vector of speed, absolute position and lane for every vehicle, sorted by position: s = (v_i, x_i, l_i), in R^(3k) [p. 52]. "Partially observable settings are out of the scope of this chapter" [p. 52].
- **State equivalence classes.** Non-automated vehicles are ordered by position, then automated ones, so that swapping two vehicles leaves the state unchanged [p. 53-54]. The thesis claims this speeds convergence (Fig. 9, multi-lane) [p. 54-55]. The authors state that the approach "may not generalize to more complex network structures" [p. 54].
- **Action.** Acceleration in [-6, 3] m/s^2 per AV. In multi-lane settings also a lane-change direction in [-1, 1], rounded to {left, stay, right} [p. 52].
- **Reward.** r(v) = max{0, ||v_des 1|| - ||v - v_des 1||}, a non-negative deviation from a desired velocity [p. 53, eq. 10]. The value of v_des is not stated in the text. The thesis says the reward must be non-negative so that early termination (collision) is punished [p. 53]. A fail-safe is optionally imposed on AVs [p. 52].
- **Training budget.** Compute is stated as AWS EC2 c4.2xlarge, 8 CPUs, 15 GB [p. 65]. Iteration counts, batch size and wall-clock are not stated in the text. Learning curves are shown in Fig. 9 and Fig. 14 [p. 55, 61].
- **Seeds.** Not stated.

### 4. Baselines
- **Ch. 4 compares only against the no-AV system** (all human IDM) and against a theoretical bound.
  - The equilibrium velocity of uniform flow in the all-human system, "which corresponds to an unstable equilibrium" [p. 57].
  - A derived upper bound V_max(n_a, n_h) = v_e_inverse((L - n_a L_veh)/n_h), described as "conjectured" [p. 57].
- No tuned classical controller is compared in Ch. 4 (see Ch. 6 for FollowerStopper and PI with Saturation). Related work contrasts RL with ramp-metering RL (Belletti) but runs nothing [p. 63].

### 5. Evaluation
- **Metric.** Average (system-level) velocity of all vehicles. Throughput, delay and TTT are not reported. The thesis itself lists "throughput, delays" as other possible objectives [p. 51].
- **Runs and statistics.** Single learning curves and space-time diagrams (Figs. 9, 11-16). No confidence intervals, no number of seeds, no tests are stated in Ch. 4.
- **Train and test separation.** None stated. The reported "emergent behaviors" are the trained policy on the same network and scenario. The chapter says generalization is future work: "Topics of future research include learning policies which generalize across a variety of traffic scenarios" [p. 64].
- The claim about hand-designed controllers failing out-of-distribution is in the Ch. 4 abstract [p. 46] but is supported only by Ch. 6.

### 6. Headline results (numbers)
- A single AV stabilizes 21 string-unstable IDM vehicles on the ring, removing stop-and-go waves [p. 57-59, Fig. 11].
- Tailgating. The AV "learns to tailgate its preceding vehicle and uses a safe distance less than that of the human drivers", so the mean velocity exceeds the all-human equilibrium velocity [p. 58]. The AV stays below the derived upper bound [p. 58].
- Strings of AVs (22 vehicles, 230 m ring): 3 AVs settle at 3.70 m/s; 11 AVs at 4.44 m/s [p. 59, Fig. 12].
- Multi-lane (44 vehicles, 6 AVs): "average velocity is 3.66 m/s, again exceeding the 3.45 m/s uniform flow equilibrium velocity" [p. 58-60]. This is about +6 % (INTERPRETATION: my own arithmetic, not stated by the thesis).
- Figure-eight: 1 AV + 13 humans reach 8.75 m/s versus 5.48 m/s with no AVs ("60% improvement"); 14 of 14 AVs reach 14 m/s ("255% improvement") [p. 60-61].
- Chapter 4 reports that, relative to the all-human baseline, these gains happen even with human acceleration noise [p. 58].

### 7. Robustness and realism
- Gaussian acceleration noise N(0, 0.2) in the human models [p. 52, p. 58].
- No sensor noise, communication delay, detection error, compliance variation or heterogeneous human drivers in Ch. 4. The thesis says real life has "different human drivers exhibiting differing levels of aggression or timidity... accidents" and that this chapter "sets the seed for further research in more complex settings" [p. 47-48].
- No sim-to-real or field test. Motivation cites field studies by others: Stern et al. (2017) "reduction in fuel consumption of 40%" with one AV in ring traffic; Barth and Boriboonsomsin: "13% reduction in fuel consumption" on SR-91 [p. 61].
- The author notes that the learned behaviors need "more investigation... to understand the resulting learned behaviors and policies and thereby take steps towards a real-world deployment" [p. 61].

### 8. Stated limitations and future work
- Fully observed state, centralized policy, closed networks [p. 51-52].
- State-equivalence ordering "is an open problem" for networks without a clean absolute distance [p. 54].
- Future work: generalization across scenarios, other system-level and local objectives (energy, comfort, "fairness"), "mixed control of automated vehicles and infrastructure", "effects at the scale of city networks", variable numbers of vehicles via pooling or recurrent policies [p. 64-65].

## Chapter 6: Flow and the ring-road controller benchmark (the only RL-vs-tuned-controller comparison)

### 1. Problem and network
- Library description (SUMO + TraCI + rllab / Ray RLlib) [p. 91-99]. Building-block networks: ring, multi-lane ring, figure-eight, loop with merge, intersections, "highway networks with pre-defined in-flows" listed as supported [p. 100]. Only the ring is benchmarked against explicit controllers.
- Case study (6.6): single-lane ring, 22 vehicles, one autonomous, ring length 180-380 m (varying density). "The vehicles are each 5m long" [p. 104].

### 2. Simulator and models
- SUMO with Flow extensions. Human vehicles use IDM with Gaussian acceleration noise N(0, 0.2) [p. 104]. Fail-safe: the "final position rule" plus SUMO's safety behaviour [p. 118-119].
- Thesis admits SUMO limitations for RL: built-in models use a minimum time headway tau, no time delays, and are "calibrated for a simulation timestep of 1.0 seconds" [p. 112]. Flow adds delays and smaller steps [p. 112, p. 114].
- Capacity drop: not modelled or mentioned. Calibration against field data: not stated (the ring setup follows the published Sugiyama experiment [p. 104]).

### 3. RL formulation
- TRPO, linear feature baselines, gamma 0.999, step 0.01 [p. 96]. Stabilizing-ring MLP hidden layer (3,3), tanh; GRU policy hidden layer (5,) [p. 96].
- **Partially observed**: the AV sees only its own speed, the leader's speed and the relative position to its leader [p. 104].
- Reward: average velocity of vehicles in the network, sometimes with a penalty on accelerations or lane changes [p. 103].
- Single AV. Trained over varying densities (ring lengths 180-380 m); "the gray regions indicate the test-time densities" [p. 104, p. 109-110]. Horizon 150 to 300 s [p. 104]. Seeds and iteration counts: not stated.

### 4. Baselines (explicit controllers, and how tuned)
- FollowerStopper (Stern et al. 2017), "desired velocity fixed at 4.15 m/s". Tuning: "we selected the largest fixed velocity which successfully stabilizes the ring at 260m" [p. 105].
- PI with Saturation (Stern et al. 2017), with parameters "calibrated for 260 m ring roads (as described in Stern et al.)" [p. 109]. Parameter values come from the Stern paper, not tuned here [p. 105-106].
- Human IDM (no control) as the baseline [p. 105].
- No ALINEA, MPC or other RL baseline.

### 5. Evaluation
- Metric: average velocity; the plotted velocity is "for the final 100s of simulation time" [p. 109]. Fig. 27 reports "averaged over ten runs for each of the tested densities" [p. 110]. No CI, no statistical test stated.
- Train and test: a train density range and out-of-range test densities (gray regions) [p. 109-110]. Also test with noise while trained without noise [p. 109].

### 6. Headline results
- GRU and MLP policies "match the uniform flow speed very closely for all trained densities" and generalize outside the training range [p. 106, p. 109-110].
- PI with Saturation "is only capable of properly performing at densities less than or equal to the density at which it was calibrated" [p. 106].
- FollowerStopper "can only stabilize a 260m ring road to a speed of 4.15 m/s, well below the 4.82 m/s uniform flow velocity" [p. 106-107].
- The thesis's claim: "deep RL can be used to learn a controller which performs better than state-of-the-art hand-designed controllers for the given setting, in terms of velocity" [p. 109].

### 7. Robustness / realism
- Robustness is claimed as independence from a density-specific calibration; "it does not rely on external calibration of parameters that is specific to a particular traffic setting, such as density" [p. 108].
- Trained without noise, tested with noise: policies "successfully stabilized settings with human model noise during test time" [p. 109].
- No sensor-noise, delay (apart from the library feature), field or sim-to-real test. The summary hopes microsimulation will be "the last step before field implementation" [p. 113].
- The thesis notes that the fully observed MLP case was not run, "we do not explore the fully observed setting" [p. 109].

### 8. Stated limitations and future work
- Future: other RL algorithms and non-RL methods in Flow, because of "poor sample efficiency of current RL methods"; evaluating safety "in addition to efficiency"; using Flow to design interpretable controllers with provable properties; more network types [p. 113].
- Ch. 11: challenges are safety, robustness, behavioral drift (humans change behaviour once automation appears), "learning in the absence of human behavior models" [p. 224]. Opportunities: structured/hierarchical/multi-agent/model-based RL, task and sample complexity, high-performance simulation [p. 226-227].

## 9. INTERPRETATION (labelled; not claims of the thesis)

### (a) Where the gain physically comes from, and would it survive a realistic capacity drop of 2-11 %?
- **Ring (Ch. 4 and 6).** The gain is removal of string-unstable stop-and-go waves in a closed loop with no inflow, outflow or bottleneck. One AV acts as a damper, plus it tailgates to use space, so mean speed rises toward or above the uniform-flow equilibrium [p. 57-58]. In the thesis's own words the setting has "no accidents, traffic bottlenecks, work zones" in the phantom-jam discussion [p. 37]. The gain is bounded by how much of the speed loss is due to waves. In a high-capacity-drop setting, wave suppression and bottleneck metering both matter. In a mild-drop setting (2-11 %), the upper bound on what any wave or bottleneck controller can recover is small. Nothing here speaks to that.
- **Figure-eight (+60 % with 1 AV, +255 % with all AVs).** The baseline is SUMO's right-of-way model, which "induces a stop-sign-like intersection behavior, generating queues" [p. 62]. The AV spaces the human vehicles so none stop. The large percentage is relative to a baseline that is poor by construction (conflicting flows with a stop-like rule, 14 vehicles in a 402 m loop). This is a simulator-rule effect, not a capacity-drop effect.
- **Would it survive a 2-11 % capacity drop?** The thesis gives no evidence either way, because no capacity drop, ramp, merge or open demand exists in its experiments. My inference: the reported percentages are not transferable. They come from instability or conflict artefacts of closed toy networks, so they say nothing about what remains once the physical headroom is only a few percent. This is consistent with the candidate's own finding that large gains came from a plant with a 40-50 % drop.

### (b) What is weak in the evaluation
- No capacity-drop or bottleneck model; mean speed as the only metric (not throughput, delay including queues, TTT). The reward is mean speed, which the AV can raise by tailgating, i.e. by reducing its own gap, which the thesis reports as emergent [p. 58].
- Ch. 4: baseline is only "no AV" and a conjectured bound; no tuned classical controller; no seeds, CIs or tests; no train/test split; fully observed centralized policy with absolute positions, which is not deployable.
- Ch. 6: the explicit controllers are calibrated at one density (260 m) while the RL policy is trained across the density range, so the comparison favours RL by design. FollowerStopper's desired speed was chosen as the largest stabilizing one at 260 m [p. 105], not tuned per density. "Ten runs" per density [p. 110] but no intervals. Only velocity is compared.
- IDM parameters are "typical values" [p. 50] with no fit to field data; SUMO's own step/delay limitations are acknowledged [p. 112] but the experimental step is not reported.
- Percent improvements (60 %, 255 %) are quoted against weak or unstable baselines.

### (c) What an innovative follow-up could be, given the candidate's finding
These are my suggestions, not the thesis's.
1. **Use the thesis's one transferable idea: compare RL against calibrated classical controllers across a range of conditions, and report where each fails** (the thesis shows the hand-designed controller degrades off its calibration density [p. 106, p. 109]). Transfer: tune ALINEA / PI-ALINEA on one demand or capacity-drop level, then test across levels and report the regime map. Gain claims then rest on adaptation and robustness, not on beating a well-tuned controller everywhere. This matches the candidate's "conditional advantages" principle.
2. **State a capacity-drop sweep as the test axis** (for example 0 %, 2 %, 5 %, 8 %, 11 %, plus the 40-50 % legacy plant as a labelled stress case), so a claimed gain is always shown against the physically available headroom. The thesis has no such axis; this would be a genuine addition.
3. **Wave suppression plus bottleneck control as a joint objective** (CAV speed commands upstream of the bottleneck to stop the waves that feed the capacity drop, alongside metering). Thesis evidence: a single AV damps waves on a ring [p. 57-59]. Whether this matters on an open bottleneck with a 5-8 % drop is untested and must be measured, not assumed.
4. **Metrics the thesis lacks**: delay including origin and ramp queues, served throughput, TTT, with paired-seed CIs and held-out seeds, so that "emergent" speed gains cannot come from tailgating or metric gaming.
5. **Partially observed, deployable policies** (the thesis already uses a 3-input observation for the ring [p. 104]) instead of absolute-position full state, and a deliberately weak-baseline check (a fair, tuned feedback controller) before any claim.
