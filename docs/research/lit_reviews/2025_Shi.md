# Review: Tianyu Shi, PhD thesis, Univ. of Toronto, 2025

Title: "Cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning Framework for Concurrent Microscopic Safe, Efficient, and Comfortable Autonomous Driving and Macroscopic Congestion Management on Freeways" [p. 1]

Source file: `text/2025_Shi_Toronto_PhD.txt`. Read in full for Ch. 1, 4, 5, 6 and the relevant parts of Ch. 2-3 (training, baselines, simulator, tables).

**Page convention.** `[p. N]` is the `=== [page N] ===` marker of the text file (PDF page). The printed page number is N minus 20 (marker 135 = printed 115). Quotes are verbatim, at most 25 words. "not stated" means I did not find it in the text. INTERPRETATION marks my own reading, not the thesis.

**What the thesis is (one paragraph).** It is a car-following / lane-change / merging thesis, not a classic traffic-control thesis. Every vehicle is a CAV driven by a DDPG/MADDPG policy. The "control" acts through the driving policy of all vehicles, not through ramp meters or speed limits. Only Ch. 5 has a macro-level controller (a scalar "cooperation factor" updated every 60 s), and it is compared with ALINEA only in the form "CoopSECRM2D + ALINEA". There is no VSL baseline, no MPC and no human-driven (HDV) traffic in any traffic-flow experiment.

Chapters: Ch. 2 SECRM (car-following), Ch. 3 SECRM2D (+ lane change), Ch. 4 CoopSECRM2D (MARL merging), Ch. 5 CoopSECRM2D-MM (macro-aware bilevel). Ch. 4 and Ch. 5 are "Manuscript submitted for possible publication", i.e. not yet peer-reviewed [p. 37]. Ch. 2 and 3 are published (TRR 2024; IET ITS 2025) [p. 37].

## Ch. 2 SECRM (car-following, not traffic control; summarised for completeness)

1. **Problem/network.** Single-lane loop, 2 vehicles (regular driving, emergency braking), plus a straight single-vehicle speed-following test [p. 65-67]. No bottleneck, no demand, no incidents except a scripted leader braking from 28 to 5 m/s [p. 79].
2. **Simulator.** SUMO via an augmented Flow middleware and TraCI [p. 73]. Parameters: "r = 0.1 s, aF = aL = bF = bL = 3 m/s2, and ε = 2 m", infinite detection range [p. 74]. The baselines IDM (δ = 4), Shladover ACC, CFM-RL (Zhu 2020) and Gipps are implemented in the authors' code [p. 67-71]. Capacity drop is not modelled or discussed. No calibration against field data (not stated).
3. **RL.** DDPG, single agent. Table 2.1: gamma 0.99, tau 0.001, actor lr 1e-4, critic lr 1e-3, buffer 1e6, batch 64, OU noise std 0.02 [p. 64]. Training: "200 episodes with a horizon of 3000 timesteps per episode", with curriculum on speed limits [p. 64-65]. Seeds not stated here. Safety is a hard action bound (analytic max safe speed), not a reward term.
4. **Baselines.** IDM, Gipps, Shladover, CFM-RL. Non-SECRM models with a target gap are run at (a) SECRM's average gap and (b) "the smallest target time-gap that does not crash" found by 0.1 s increments [p. 73]. The baselines were therefore tuned only for crash-freeness.
5. **Evaluation.** A single run per scenario in tables 2.2-2.6 (no CIs, no seeds stated) [p. 76-85].
6. **Results.** Regular driving at SECRM's gap: SECRM avg time-gap 0.6182 s, jerk 0.013; IDM 0.7102 s / 0.129 [p. 76]. At the smallest safe gap: IDM 2.2737 s, Shladover 2.1395 s, CFM-RL 1.2032 s, SECRM 0.6182 s [p. 78]. Emergency braking: CFM-RL, IDM and Shladover crash at SECRM's gap [p. 81].
7. **Robustness.** None beyond the scripted braking test.
8. **Limits.** Future work: system stability in the reward, and a lane-change module [p. 86].
9. INTERPRETATION: the short 0.6 s gap comes from the 0.1 s reaction time (a stated "futuristic value" [p. 74]) plus perfect sensing. This is the origin of the "high capacity" of the whole thesis (see 9a).

## Ch. 3 SECRM2D (single ego CAV among SUMO human-like vehicles; not traffic control)

1. **Network.** Loop network (multi-lane, effectively an infinite freeway, 25 or 50 human-driver vehicles) and the QEW / Erin Mills / Southdown interchange in SUMO [p. 114-115]. QEW training inflow of uncontrolled vehicles is 500-1000 veh/h [p. 116], i.e. no bottleneck congestion. Incidents: scripted emergency braking [p. 126, 129].
2. **Simulator/models.** SUMO + TraCI, step 0.1 s [p. 114]. Uncontrolled vehicles: "Krauss" car-following and "SL2015" lane change [p. 115]. Their parameters are not stated. Capacity drop: not modelled or measured. No field calibration.
3. **RL.** Single-agent DDPG + analytic action constraints. Table 3.1: gamma 0.99, lr 3e-4, tau 0.005, buffer 1e6, 3x256 MLP, batch 64 [p. 117]. "Five random seeds were sampled, and training was run for 1000 episodes for each seed." [p. 117] "The checkpoint that achieved the highest cumulative reward across the 5 seeds was selected for evaluation." [p. 117]. That is, the best of 5 seeds was reported.
4. **Baselines.** IDM+MOBIL, Gipps+greedy lane choice, PPO lane-change (Ye 2020), MCTS highway-exit planner (Cao 2020) [p. 123-125]. Tuning of these is not stated.
5. **Evaluation.** Loop: 5000 steps, 30 random seeds [p. 123]. Test speed limits are outside the training set (17 m/s for HVs, 34 m/s for the ego) [p. 125]. No CIs or tests.
6. **Results.** Table 3.2, normal driving: speed 32.45 m/s, jerk 0.86, crash 0 % for SECRM-2D versus 27.48 / 0.87 / 0 % for IDM+MOBIL; the PPO baseline crashes 3 %, 7 % and 21 % in the three scenarios [p. 123]. Merging (Table 3.5): SECRM-2D crash 0 %, merge miss 13 % [p. 127].
7. **Robustness.** Reaction-time dependence (Table 3.6) [p. 128]. The paper's own condition: "given accurate estimates of reaction times and braking rates of the surrounding vehicles" [p. 131]. The 0 % crash claim depends on this.
8. **Limits.** String stability of the platoon is "the subject of future work" [p. 123].
9. INTERPRETATION: baseline rows of Table 3.5 (merging) are identical to the H=5 columns of Table 3.3 (bypassing) for IDM, Gipps and PPO (18.67/1.23/13 %/31 %, etc.), while the "Mandatory Lane Change RL" crash rate differs (18 % in Table 3.3 vs 11 % in Table 3.5) [p. 127]. Either the scenarios coincide or a table was copied. Verify before citing.

## Ch. 4 CoopSECRM2D (MARL on-ramp merging, "bubble" reward sharing)

1. **Problem and network.**
   - All-CAV on-ramp merge: "where all vehicles are driven by CAVs" [p. 140]. Sections: mainline Lma, merge zone Lm, acceleration section La, ramp Lr [p. 140].
   - Synthetic network (Table 4.1) [p. 160]: main section 1031.59 m, ramp 1039.9 m, acceleration section 359.7 m, "Maximum Capacity 5538 veh/hour", horizon 200 min. Demands: main 5500, 4950, 4400, 3300, 1650 veh/h, ramp 1500 or 900 veh/h (the text says "5500 to 1650" [p. 159]). Lane count not stated for the synthetic network (INTERPRETATION: 2 lanes, see 9a).
   - QEW single on-ramp (Table 4.1) [p. 160]: main 1108.43 m, ramp 858.4 m, acceleration 301.4 m, "Maximum Capacity 8307 veh/hour", horizon 300 min, "Dynamic changing" demand (profile in Fig. 4.10, no numbers in text) [p. 161]. Detector/lane count: three lanes are mentioned in the inference section [p. 207-208].
   - Incidents: none (not stated).
   - Lane drop: a CAV that fails to merge must stop before the end of the acceleration lane. SUMO's default handling "models the vehicle as coming to a sudden stop. This behavior is unrealistic and can lead to abnormal congestion." [p. 128] The authors replace this with an analytic deceleration region for their own agents [p. 129-130].
2. **Simulator/models.**
   - SUMO [p. 158]. Time step 1 s, reaction time r = 1 s, minimum gap 4 m, acceleration range [-5, 3] m/s2, target speed 30 m/s, initial speed 27-30 m/s [p. 160].
   - Baseline "SUMO default": Krauss car-following and SL2015 lane change, "lcCooperative" 0 or 1 [p. 166, 172]. Krauss/SL2015 parameters (tau, sigma, speedFactor, etc.) are not stated.
   - Capacity drop: discussed qualitatively ("mitigate capacity drop" [p. 140]; Fig. 4.14/4.15 show flow drops). Its size is not stated and not quantified anywhere in the thesis. There is no calibration or validation against field data (not stated). The QEW geometry is real; the demand is not stated to be calibrated.
   - How "Maximum Capacity" was obtained: not stated. See INTERPRETATION 9a.
3. **RL.**
   - MADDPG ("We follow the same way to update the networks using the Multi-Agent Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (MADDPG) training strategy") [p. 158]. Decentralised POMDP; each agent sees vehicles inside a scan radius R = max(50, safe gap) m [p. 146, 154].
   - State [p. 146-147]: ego distance in section, speed, previous acceleration, section index, lane index, lateral speed, route-lane mask; for the Nfront/Nback nearest vehicles per lane: distance, speed, acceleration.
   - Action [p. 147-148]: continuous acceleration in [-aE, aE] plus discrete lane change {left, cur, right}, with analytic safety constraints (hard action bound).
   - Reward [p. 151-153]: selfish part RS = efficiency (speed vs safe target speed) + comfort (jerk squared) + discretionary lane-change + route term; plus W times the mean reward of front/back neighbours. Dynamic factor W_dyn = 1 + K |speed_adjacent - speed_current| / speed_limit (K = 0.2). Auxiliary "bubble speed loss" L = max(|Vtarget - Vbar| / Vtarget, 0.01) [p. 156-157].
   - Penetration: 100 % CAV. Single shared policy or not: not stated. Training budget (episodes, steps, updates), learning rates, number of training seeds: not stated for Ch. 4 (and Ch. 5).
4. **Baselines** [p. 166-167]: SUMO default (coop=0 and coop=1), SECRM2D (selfish), UAAP (Wang 2021, courteous), MARL-CAV (Chen 2023), and CoopSECRM2D ablations (no bubble loss, bubble speed as reward, bubble speed as loss, dynamic W). No ramp metering, VSL or MPC. How the literature baselines were tuned: not stated. W and K were chosen "through extensive experiments" [p. 156], and "a cooperation factor of 1.5 is overly conservative" [p. 162], i.e. W was tuned on the evaluation scenarios.
5. **Evaluation.**
   - Metrics: "microscore" = sum of the four reward terms (LongEff, DLcE, Comf, MLc) per vehicle over the horizon, averaged over vehicles [p. 160-161]; outflow and ramp flow (synthetic network); total travel time TTT (QEW) [p. 159].
   - Number of runs/seeds: not stated. Statistics (CI, tests): none. Train/test separation: not stated; the same networks and demand levels appear in the tuning discussion and in the results. Generalisation tests: none beyond the demand levels.
   - Whether TTT counts origin/insertion delay: not stated ("aggregate time all vehicles spend in the network", defined in Ch. 5 [p. 206]).
6. **Headline results** (Table 4.2, synthetic, ramp 1500 veh/h) [p. 168]:
   - Main 5500: outflow SUMO default (coop=0) 3616, SECRM2D 4968, CoopSECRM2D (bubble loss) 4596, dyn coop 4529. Cooperation is worse than selfish SECRM2D on outflow: "while cooperation enhances local driving behavior, it comes at the cost of reduced overall traffic throughput under high-demand conditions" [p. 169].
   - Main 4950: outflow 4429 (SECRM2D) vs 4537 (Coop bubble loss), +2.4 %. Main 4400: 4193 vs 4301, +2.6 %. Main 3300: 4162 vs 4781, +14.9 %, but SECRM2D ramp flow is only 866 of 1500 veh/h demand versus 1436 for Coop.
   - Main 1650: outflows 3068-3110 for all models (no congestion).
   - QEW dynamic demand (Table 4.3) [p. 171]: TTT SUMO default (coop=0) 1299 h, SECRM2D 979 h, MARL-CAV 988 h, CoopSECRM2D (bubble loss) 877 h, dyn coop 881 h. The text states the "minimal system TTT (877)" belongs to the bubble-loss variant.
   - The author lists the finding that "under extreme demand conditions, selfish behaviors may lead to higher overall flow performance" [p. 139].
7. **Robustness/realism.** None (no noise, no communication loss, no HDVs, no field data, no sim-to-real). Only demand levels and scan range (50/100/150 m/dynamic) were varied [p. 164-166].
8. **Stated limits/future work** [p. 176]: bottleneck information outside the local scan, optimising W by a bilevel framework to maximise outflow, and "comparing the performance of cooperative CAV models with infrastructure control, such as ramp metering". Also: dynamic cooperation "can also limit the speed advantage of faster lanes, potentially reducing total traffic throughput" [p. 176].
9. INTERPRETATION: see the common section below.

## INTERPRETATION (my reading, not the thesis)

### (a) Where does the reported gain come from, and would it survive a realistic 2-11 % capacity drop?

1. **Driver-model change dominates the large numbers.**
   - All vehicles use the SECRM safe-gap rule with r = 1 s, minimum gap 4 m, 30 m/s [p. 160], and vehicle length 5 m (Fig. 3.10 caption [p. 123]). With the thesis's own steady-state gap formula (w r + eps) [p. 122], the steady spacing is 34 m + 5 m = 39 m, i.e. a headway of 1.3 s, or 3600/1.3 = 2769 veh/h/lane.
   - 3 x 2769 = 8308 and 2 x 2769 = 5538, matching the "Maximum Capacity" entries 8307 and 5538 [p. 160]. The thesis does not state this derivation, so I am inferring it from the numbers. It is a strong numerical match but is not confirmed; please verify.
   - If right, the "capacity" is an analytic property of the controller's safe-gap rule, not a measured bottleneck capacity of a calibrated human-driver plant. The SUMO baseline is labelled "low-capacity driving model" [p. 210] and the text says "SECRM model has lower average time gap" [p. 172].
   - The 25-38 % TTT reductions versus SUMO default (table above) are therefore mostly a change of driver model, not learned cooperation. Supporting evidence: at 0.6x demand, where the thesis says "all models achieve similar performance" in flow [p. 213], TTT still differs by 33 % (830 h vs 557 h).
2. **Baseline artefact.** SUMO's lane-end behaviour of a vehicle that fails to merge ("sudden stop", "abnormal congestion" [p. 128]) is fixed only for the authors' agents, so the SUMO-default arms probably suffer a teleport/stop-type artefact at the lane drop. This is analogous to your concern about capacity drops created by simulator artefacts. I could not tell from the thesis how much of the SUMO arms' loss it explains.
3. **The learned-cooperation increment is small.** Against the strongest all-CAV comparator at full demand, MM (infer) is -3.6 % (vs CoopSECRM2D coop=1) and -2.8 % (vs +ALINEA) in TTT, from single runs. At 5500 veh/h in the synthetic network, cooperation reduces outflow by 7.5 % relative to selfish SECRM2D (4596 vs 4968) [p. 168-169]. The mechanisms the thesis describes are: (i) fewer ramp-blocking events (selfish SECRM2D serves 866 of 1500 ramp veh/h at main 3300), (ii) slower-lane / faster-lane speed equalisation, (iii) macro-weighted W.
4. **Survival under a 2-11 % capacity drop: I do not know.** The thesis never states the size of any capacity drop. A 3-6 % TTT gain from one run without CI is within the range of the drop sizes you consider, so I would not treat it as evidence that RL beats a well-tuned feedback controller on a realistic plant. The only feedback-metering comparison (ALINEA over CoopSECRM2D drivers) gives -2.9 % to +7.2 % TTT versus no metering with the same drivers (ALINEA unspecified, untuned as far as stated), which is consistent with your finding that metering does not beat no control by 10 %, but on a different plant.

### (b) Weak points in the evaluation

- No seeds, CIs or tests for Ch. 4-5; best-of-5-seeds checkpoint selection by training reward in Ch. 3 [p. 117].
- Hyperparameters (W = 1, K = 0.2, cmb = 0.2) are chosen on the evaluation scenarios [p. 156, 162, 191, 198]; train/test separation for Ch. 4-5 is not stated.
- Own metric (microscore) is the training reward, so it is partly self-referential; outflow/TTT are the only independent measures, and ramp queue/insertion delay and served throughput are not reported (TTT definition unclear).
- Baselines: SUMO default untuned (parameters not stated) and shown to have low capacity; ALINEA untuned/unspecified and not tested on the neutral driver model; no VSL, no MPC, no tuned feedback arm.
- 100 % CAV, perfect V2V and sensing, 1 s control step, 0.1-1 s reaction time; no HDV heterogeneity, no noise, no capacity-drop calibration.
- The "bilevel optimisation" upper level is not specified as an optimiser (algorithm not stated), so it is closer to a scheduler of one scalar every 60 s than a solved bilevel problem.
- Inference module checked only in plots, at full observability of CAVs; the "infer" variant beating the loop-detector variant [p. 217] is unexplained and could be noise.
- Several numerical inconsistencies (see above).

### (c) Innovative follow-ups, given your finding (realistic 5-8 % drop, metering and constant control do not reach 10 %)

These are suggestions drawn from the gaps above, not claims of novelty (I have not searched literature).
1. **Same-driver ablation as a protocol.** Re-run this kind of comparison with the baseline and the learned controller sharing the same car-following parameters, and report the share of the gain that remains. This isolates what RL adds beyond a driver-model change; the thesis cannot show it.
2. **Supervisory scalar on a safe micro layer.** The one idea in the thesis that transfers: a low-dimensional macro-aware parameter (one factor updated every 60 s) over a fixed safe low-level policy. On your plant this could be a learned schedule of a VSL/CAV-speed/metering set-point, with headroom measured against a tuned lookup-table controller (as in your CLAUDE.md) and pre-registered regimes.
3. **Where RL could still matter on a realistic plant.** Low-penetration mixed autonomy (explicitly the thesis's own future work [p. 229]), because the capacity drop is set by HDVs; test whether CAV actions reduce the drop size itself rather than raise a designed capacity.
4. **Detector-free state estimation under partial penetration.** The thesis tests inference only at 100 % penetration. A numeric error and closed-loop sensitivity study (5-20 % penetration, noise, dropouts) against loop detectors would be a measurable, falsifiable contribution.
5. **Neutral reporting.** Report TTT including insertion/ramp queues, served throughput, tuned feedback baselines per regime, paired seeds with CIs, so a null (KILL-type) result on a 5-8 % plant is itself publishable as an operating map.
