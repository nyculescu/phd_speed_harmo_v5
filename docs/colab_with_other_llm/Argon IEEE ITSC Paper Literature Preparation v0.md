### 3. Gap Map & Web of Science Search Refinements

#### Honest Assessment of Prior Work on the "Information Ladder"

Based on our verification of the literature, **no prior paper was found that decomposes an incident-information ladder (no info $\rightarrow$ presence $\rightarrow$ type $\rightarrow$ bias-corrected hindsight oracle) for freeway mainline signal metering.**

Here is what the literature *has* done versus what remains open:

* **Mainline signal metering at stationary bottlenecks is established:** Papageorgiou et al. [2], Spiliopoulou et al. [3], and Tympakianaki et al. [1] established that placing green-red traffic signals directly across the motorway mainstream upstream of a toll-plaza merge or a work-zone lane drop—driven by ALINEA or PI-ALINEA occupancy/speed feedback—prevents capacity drop and maximizes throughput. However, in [1]–[3], the bottleneck geometry and capacity reduction are **fixed and known** (a planned work zone or toll plaza).
* **Online set-point adaptation without explicit incident detection exists:** Smaragdis and Papageorgiou [21] (AD-ALINEA) recognized that critical occupancy shifts under non-stationary conditions (weather, incidents) and proposed extremum-seeking set-point adaptation using only local loop measurements. This belongs to your **L0 (no explicit incident feed)** tier.
* **Incident-responsive control has focused on on-ramps or VSLs, assuming a single information level:** Studies such as Sheu and Chang [11], Dabiri and Kulcsár [12], and Wu et al. [13] design incident-responsive controllers (Primarily for ramp metering). Crucially, these papers compare their incident-aware controller against either *no control* or *fixed-parameter baseline control*, rather than stepping through granular levels of incident information (e.g., binary presence vs. severity/type classification) against a strong suite of always-on feedback controllers (PI-ALINEA + adaptive set-point).
* **Hindsight oracles in traffic control are rarely corrected for selection bias:** In traffic simulation studies, "optimal" or "oracle" benchmarks are typically tuned or selected on the same stochastic simulation realizations (seeds) used for evaluation. As shown in decision analysis (Smith and Winkler [16]) and algorithm portfolio evaluation (Cameron et al. [14]), picking the best parameter or plan per instance on noisy realizations inflates the upper bound due to selection on noise (the optimizer's curse). Applying cross-fitting (sample splitting across paired seeds, analogous to van Hasselt [15]) to bracket the true hindsight headroom in traffic microsimulation addresses a clear methodological gap.

### 4. Introduction Draft (~450 words, IEEE Style)

Non-recurrent disturbances—such as lane blockages, vehicle breakdowns, and localized slowdowns—account for a large share of freeway delay and reduce bottleneck discharge rates through the capacity-drop phenomenon. While on-ramp metering is widely deployed to regulate merging demand, it cannot control traffic already on the freeway mainstream when an incident occurs far from an entrance ramp or when mainstream demand alone exceeds the remaining bottleneck capacity. In high-demand corridors such as bridge approaches, tunnels, toll plazas, and severe lane drops, **mainline (mainstream) metering**—placing traffic signals directly across all freeway lanes upstream of the bottleneck—provides a direct mechanism to regulate inflow and prevent breakdown [1]–[5]. Field installations at the San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge and the Baltimore Harbor Tunnel have demonstrated the operational feasibility of mainline signals [4], and simulation studies have extended mainstream signal control to freeway merges and work-zone lane drops [1], [2], [6].

For stationary bottlenecks, closed-loop feedback controllers borrowed from ramp metering have proven highly effective. Integral regulators such as ALINEA [10] and proportional-integral extensions such as PI-ALINEA [9] maintain bottleneck occupancy or speed near its critical threshold without requiring a detailed model of upstream demand. These local feedback laws have been successfully applied both to Mainstream Traffic Flow Control (MTFC) via variable speed limits [7], [8], [20] and to mainstream traffic signals at work zones and toll plazas [1]–[3]. Furthermore, adaptive schemes such as AD-ALINEA estimate the critical set point online from traffic measurements [21]. In parallel, the incident-management literature has developed incident-responsive ramp controllers that switch control laws or schedule parameters when a non-recurring event occurs [11]–[13].

However, deploying an incident-responsive architecture requires real-time incident detection and classification, which introduce latency and false alarms. A practical engineering question remains open: **how much incident information does a mainline metering signal actually need beyond strong always-on feedback?** Specifically, it is unclear whether binary incident presence is sufficient, whether classifying the incident type adds meaningful throughput or delay benefits, and how close these practical information tiers come to the theoretical headroom of full per-incident hindsight.

To answer this question, we evaluate a four-level incident information ladder at a microsimulated four-to-one freeway bottleneck in SUMO [17] subject to stochastic non-recurring incidents (bottleneck slowdowns and single-lane crawls). We compare: (L0) always-on control without incident information (fixed rate, integral feedback, PI-ALINEA [9], and online set-point adaptation [21]); (L1) binary incident presence switching; (L2) incident-type plan switching; and (L3) per-incident hindsight oracles. Because selecting the best control plan per incident realization on noisy microsimulation runs inflates oracle performance via the optimizer's curse [14], [16], we bracket the hindsight ceiling using both an in-sample oracle and a cross-fitted oracle [15].

The main contributions of this paper are:

* **An incident-information ladder decomposition for mainline signal metering:** We isolate the marginal value of binary incident presence (L1) and incident-type classification (L2) relative to strong always-on feedback baselines (L0) across multiple demand and incident regimes, confirming a 16.2 % gain for incident-type knowledge on out-of-sample simulation seeds in one operating regime while showing where binary presence alone yields less than 10 % improvement.

### 5. Background Section Draft (~400 words, IEEE Style)

#### A. Mainline Metering and Feedback Controllers

Mainline metering regulates traffic directly on the freeway mainstream using standard green-amber-red signal heads across all lanes upstream of a capacity bottleneck [1], [4]. Unlike variable speed limits in general MTFC [7], [8], mainstream traffic signals enforce hard discharge limits through minimum red times and amber clearance intervals, holding back queues in a multi-lane storage zone so that vehicles merge and accelerate smoothly through the bottleneck throat [1]–[3]. Under Level 0 (L0, no explicit incident feed), mainline signals can operate at a fixed metering rate or via closed-loop feedback. Classical ALINEA [10] uses integral (I) feedback on downstream occupancy, whereas PI-ALINEA [9] adds a proportional term that accelerates recovery when the bottleneck is located further downstream of the signal [1], [9]. Because an incident alters the fundamental diagram of the bottleneck, fixed set points may become suboptimal [20]; online set-point estimators (AD-ALINEA style [21]) attempt to track shifts in critical occupancy using only loop measurements. In contrast, incident-scheduled controllers (L1 and L2) explicitly switch control plans or parameters upon receiving an external incident flag [12].

#### B. Incident Detection Timing and Driver Compliance

The practical utility of L1 and L2 control depends on two field imperfections: detection latency and driver non-compliance. Automatic incident detection algorithms must balance detection delay against false alarm rates; because shockwaves propagate rapidly at a four-to-one merge, a detection delay of even one to two minutes allows queue formation and capacity drop to set in before the mainline signal switches plans. Furthermore, drivers encountering temporary or mainline metering signals do not comply perfectly. Field evaluations of ramp and work-zone metering signals report measurable red-light violation rates that vary with queue wait times and enforcement [18], [19]. Evaluating mainline signal control therefore requires explicit microscopic modeling of amber-phase braking decisions and red-light runners.

#### C. Honest Headroom in Stochastic Microsimulation

To judge whether L1 or L2 extracts most of the available benefit, researchers typically compare them against a hindsight "oracle" (L3) that selects the best control plan for each incident realization. In microscopic traffic simulators such as SUMO [17], vehicle arrivals, lane changes, and incident timings are stochastic. Even when attempting to use Common Random Numbers (paired seeds), switching a traffic signal alters downstream vehicle trajectories and desynchronizes microscopic interactions, creating substantial realization-specific noise. When an oracle selects the best-performing plan on the same simulation run used to evaluate its performance, it exploits lucky noise realizations—a phenomenon known in decision analysis as the *optimizer's curse* [16] and in algorithm benchmarking as *Virtual Best Solver (VBS) bias* [14]. To prevent this selection bias from inflating the apparent headroom, selection and evaluation must be decoupled across independent noise realizations [15]. By pairing an in-sample oracle (upper bound) with a cross-fitted oracle that selects plans on held-out seeds (lower bound), we obtain an honest interval for the maximum achievable value of incident information.
