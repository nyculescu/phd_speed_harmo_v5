Headway Control Lab

Automated cars can keep shorter or longer gaps to the car ahead. This page shows how a controller picks that gap for each automated car, how the project tests whether a learned controller beats tuned rules, and what the tests have found so far.

2 · How the project works

From a plant to one sentence

Each step is fixed before the data it judges. Click through the five steps.

A simulated freeway with a written record of what it is good for

The thesis plant (r2m3) runs in SUMO 1.27.1 at 0.1-s steps: 6.8 km, three lanes, two on-ramps, 50 % automated cars, demand 4,090-6,000 veh/h, steady or with a slowdown (X1 downstream of ramp 2, X2 between the ramps).

Its V&V record lists 28 requirements. Today 6 are met, 13 partial, 7 open gaps and 2 stated limits. Every open item becomes a numbered limit (L1-L27) that each claim sentence must carry. The plant is called valid for its intended use; it is never called proven.

Who sets the gaps

No control
every automated car keeps 1.6 s
Fixed rule (p333)
0.8 s for every car, always
Tuned rules
a grid of 70 rules, and rules tuned by CMA-ES for each slowdown, all on separate seeds
Per-CAV PPO
one learned policy picks each car's gap every 10 s from what that car sees; 5 independent learners

A command only applies behind another automated car within 150 m, seen on two updates in a row. Longer gaps open at most 0.2 s per second, so a command cannot force hard braking.

Fresh random traffic for every decision

A seed fixes the random traffic of one run. Every arm runs on the same seeds, so differences are paired. Round 2 uses separate blocks for tuning, choosing and testing; the test block is opened once.

How a comparison is read

For each seed, take the policy's value minus the opponent's. The effect is the median of those differences over the opponent's median, with a bootstrap confidence interval. A cell is won (reading T2) when at least 4 of 5 learned policies have at least 10 % less close-following than every counting rule, the interval excludes 0, and delay stays within 2 %.

The decision map uses a simpler tile rule. Move the sliders to see it work.

W

Sentences written before the data

Each slowdown cell gets one of three sentences, chosen by the reading, never written after it:

W
the policies beat every counting rule, including one tuned by CMA-ES
G
they beat the older rule grid only, not the CMA-ES rule
N
they did not beat the strongest rules; the claim closes there

Against no control, a separate bar applies: delay at least 10 % lower with the interval excluding 0, and close-following not made worse.

4 · The open question

When the disturbance is not the one a rule was tuned for

Hypothesis for later work (untested)

A rule tuned for a known slowdown at a known place loses part of its advantage when the disturbance moves or changes. A learned per-car policy reacts to what each car sees, so it may lose less. The disturbed-plants chapter will test this, with each disturbance fixed before any control result.

Two scenes show the mechanism. The zone rule sets gaps by road position, tuned in this toy for X1. The adaptive rule reacts to traffic 100-400 m ahead. It is hand-written, not learned, so it stands in for what a policy could condition on.

Toy comparison

Runs all four controllers on 3 seeds with the demand and disturbances set above, in background threads. About 10-30 s.

In our own runs of this toy, the hand-written adaptive rule did not beat the fixed rule in any condition. Adaptation has to be learned and tested, not assumed. That is what round 2 and the disturbed plants are for.

What this page is. An explainer. The road is a toy in the shape of the thesis plant (IDM drivers and simple lane changes in JavaScript, 0.2-s steps). Its controllers copy the actuator rules (four gap levels, 10-s decisions, V2V gate, 0.2 s/s gap opening) but not the trained policies.

What it is not. Evidence. The results section quotes the project's stored readings; everything else on the page is illustration. Round 2 decides the claim against tuned rules.
