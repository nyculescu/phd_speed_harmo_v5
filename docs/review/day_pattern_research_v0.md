# Tuned feedback leaves daily-pattern learning little headroom

Synthesis of the five research notes in `docs/review/day_pattern_notes/` (compiled 2026-10-04). No sources, numbers, titles or authors were added beyond those notes.

The notes do not support a large headroom for a controller that learns daily demand patterns on top of tuned freeway feedback control. They found **no study that classifies the day type early and then selects a freeway ramp-metering or VSL plan, tested against a tuned feedback controller with an effect size**. Where learning-based or predictive ramp metering is compared with a *tuned* ALINEA or PI-ALINEA, the surviving gains are about **0–4 % (median), with per-seed spreads from −21 % to +5 %**. One reinforcement-learning (RL) controller is 0.6 % *worse* than ALINEA. One reported "three-times-ALINEA" MPC gain shrinks to about 0.4 % once ALINEA's gain is set to its own TTS-best value. All of these are computations in the notes from two or three papers, not a meta-analysis. Day types themselves are mostly predictable from the calendar (about 3–6 clusters: Mon–Thu, Friday, weekend/holiday, sometimes school periods). A calendar lookup table therefore captures most of what a learner could add. The days a learner could add value on (incidents, events, unusually heavy demand) are the hardest to recognise early: the one quantified source reports **27.5 % misclassification at 08:00**, possibly from a simulation study. Two physical facts make the headroom question fragile in both directions. Counted volumes at or below an active bottleneck are clipped at the queue-discharge rate, so count-calibrated plants understate heavy days and erase headroom. Plants with an *assumed* capacity drop bake headroom in. The notes found **no published SUMO configuration that reproduces both ≥ 1,600 veh/h/lane discharge and a 3–18 % capacity drop**. The practical implication, which is an inference, is that the thesis should expect a KILL at the 10 % headroom gate unless heavy-demand days and a field-fitted capacity drop are both represented. It should design comparators (a day-type lookup table, a tuned feedback controller, and an MPC with a forecast fitted on separate days) that make that KILL cheap and early.

## Day types are mostly calendar-known, and early recognition is weak where it matters

Clustering daily freeway flow profiles is an established, monitoring-oriented literature. Guardiola, Leon and Mallor project 1-minute daily flow curves from the **I-94 freeway, Twin Cities, 2004–2011** into a functional-PCA score space. They then use the scores for clustering, outlier detection and control charts that flag changes in traffic patterns, not for control ([Guardiola, Leon and Mallor, 2014](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/transb/v65y2014icp119-136.html)) [VERIFY: abstract only]. Chiou models daily trajectories as a mixture of stochastic processes on a Taiwan NH5 loop (2009, 15-min flows, 70 training and 14 test days) and finds **three clusters**: weekends and holidays (about 90 % of members, with *higher* flow on this recreational route), Mon–Thu (lowest variability), and mostly Fridays (high variability after 18:00) ([Chiou, 2012](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1301.2399)) [VERIFY: abstract and summariser; tables not checked by hand]. Snippets with uncertain attribution report partitions such as Mon/Fri, Tue–Thu and Sat/Sun, a knee-chosen K = 3, and a six-type solution separating school from non-school periods ([Aimsun blog](https://www.aimsun.com/innovation-blog/patterns-from-traffic-data/); [arXiv 2011.05073](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.05073v1)) [VERIFY: snippet; attribution uncertain]. Another snippet reports that daily profile shape differs significantly by day of week *and* by season ([Sapienza/UWE repository item](https://iris.uniroma1.it/handle/11573/1723407)) [VERIFY: snippet; attribution uncertain]. Pre-classifying working vs non-working days before clustering is reported to improve clusters, which is attributed to Weijermars and van Berkum ([Academia.edu copy](https://www.academia.edu/20745533/Analyzing_highway_flow_patterns_using_cluster_analysis)) [VERIFY: title only; the statement's source document unconfirmed].

**[Inference]** About three calendar-aligned types form a robust baseline partition. Because clusters and calendar labels largely coincide, most of a day type's information is *known in advance*. The marginal value of data-driven typing lies in outlier days (incidents, weather, events), which are exactly the days that are hard to pre-classify. The notes found no source that quantifies cluster stability across seasons or years.

Early identification is the weak link. The only time-resolved accuracy figure is Chiou's: the average classification error "decreases with τ, ranging from **27.5 % at 8:00 to 7.7 % at 20:00**" ([Chiou, 2012](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1301.2399)) [VERIFY: summariser]. The summariser attributed these numbers to the paper's *simulation study*, not necessarily to the real NH5 data, so the table must be checked before the figure is relied on. **[Inference]** If roughly one day in four is mis-typed at the morning shoulder, hard plan switching would apply the wrong plan on many days. That argues for soft, probabilistic blending or parameter scheduling under a feedback layer. Because the AM peak is decided before about 08:00–09:00, the useful identification window is short.

On the control side, fixed-time or time-of-day (TOD) ramp metering is the oldest off-line strategy. It sets rates per period from historical demand without real-time measurement ([Sanandaji et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.13992)) [VERIFY: introduction read via pdftotext; not read in full]. The published "feedback beats TOD" comparisons almost always use the *existing* or untuned fixed-time plan. In I-405 microsimulation, coordinated algorithms beat "the current fixed-time control" and ALINEA ([eScholarship 082963mt](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/082963mt)) [VERIFY: snippet; authors, year and numbers not retrieved]. On I-12 Baton Rouge, ALINEA vs fixed-time cut average travel time by **2 s eastbound and 20 s westbound**, with the baseline unknown and so no percentage ([LTRC, 2017](https://www.ltrc.lsu.edu/pdf/2017/FR_570.pdf)) [VERIFY: snippet only; simulation vs field unconfirmed]. A Riyadh VISSIM study found that metering "significantly improved" upstream flow only at **110 % of base demand**, not at 80 % or 100 % ([IranDoc listing](https://doaji.irandoc.ac.ir/home/article?id=155951)) [VERIFY: snippet; authors and venue unconfirmed]. For VSL, the notes found only a TOD work-zone scheme ([Kang and Chang, 2006](https://attap.umd.edu/?p=662)) [VERIFY: abstract only], with no TOD-VSL vs feedback-VSL effect size, and no comparison of any day-type layer against SWARM, HERO or PI-ALINEA.

The closest family to "learning from previous days" is iterative learning control (ILC). Xiang et al. assume repetitive 07:00–10:00 peaks on a service-station CTM and report that ILC reaches ground-truth-MPC performance within **about 1–2 days** under a 20 % parameter mismatch. Their comparators are no control and an MPC with the true model, **not ALINEA** ([Xiang et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.11108v1)) [VERIFY: automated summary of the HTML, not read line by line]. The two notes report inconsistent TTS figures from this preprint: one summariser gave uncontrolled 359.04 vs GT-MPC 359.27 (control worse than none), while the other note gives 358.49 vs 344.64 for the 0.8× underestimation case. Neither should be used until the paper is checked. **[Inference]** ILC needs repetition *within* a type, so day typing is a prerequisite for ILC on mixed calendars; the notes did not see this stated in a source.

## Counted volumes clip heavy days at the queue-discharge rate

"Demand" and "volume served" diverge at an active bottleneck and at every segment downstream of it. McTrans states that "an active bottleneck has a demand greater than capacity, but the actual volume served in this location is metered by its capacity". Its worked HCS example has 7,500 pc/h of demand and a 7 % queue-discharge drop, giving 6,285 pc/h served. Downstream counts there understate demand by **about 16 %**, the notes' arithmetic, and a hidden downstream bottleneck with true d/c = 1.04 looks uncongested ([Sasahara, 2021](https://mctrans.ce.ufl.edu/analyzing-active-and-hidden-bottlenecks/)) [VERIFY: extracted]. FHWA's 2019 microsimulation guidance tells analysts that "care should be taken to ensure that the count measures demand and not capacity". It adds that counts should start before congestion onset and end after all congestion has dissipated ([FHWA, 2019](https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/fhwahop18036/chapter2.htm)) [VERIFY: extracted]. FHWA's 2004 guidance warns that microsimulation results are "highly sensitive to the amount by which the demand exceeds the capacity". Its forward-direction example has 5,000 veh/h of demand against 4,000 veh/h of capacity, a **20 % reduction** downstream ([FHWA, 2004](https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/trafficanalysistools/tat_vol3/sectapp_f.htm)) [VERIFY: extracted; 2004 version, partly superseded]. The HCM-era freeway method requires d/c < 1 in the first and last intervals of the analysis window ([FHWA TAT Vol. II, App. A](https://ops.fhwa.dot.gov/trafficanalysistools/tat_vol2/sectapp_a.htm)) [VERIFY: extracted; HCM 2000-era text, possibly outdated]. The HCM 6th/7th edition text itself was **not accessed**, so no chapter wording should be cited from these notes.

The physics behind the plateau is well documented. At two Toronto-area bottlenecks, queue discharge "can be **10 % lower** than the flow measured prior to the queue's formation" and is nearly stationary ([Cassidy and Bertini, 1999](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/transb/v33y1999i1p25-42.html)) [VERIFY: abstract only]. Hall and Agyemang-Duah report "roughly a **6 percent** reduction in maximum flow rates after the onset of congestion", and that capacity "can only be measured in a bottleneck, and not in a queue" ([Hall and Agyemang-Duah, 1991](https://onlinepubs.trb.org/Onlinepubs/trr/1991/1320/1320-012.pdf)) [SAGE, open TRB copy] [VERIFY: abstract and background read]. Yuan, Knoop and Hoogendoorn place discharge 5–20 % below pre-queue capacity in the literature and report a drop of "around **26 %**" when the speed in congestion is 0 km/h on the Dutch A4/A12 ([Yuan, Knoop and Hoogendoorn, 2015](https://research.tudelft.nl/files/75552687/2491_08.pdf)) [SAGE] [VERIFY: snippet only]. Newell's cumulative-curve theory "works best under over-saturated conditions" and gives queue = A(t) − D(t) directly ([Newell, 1993](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/transb/v27y1993i4p289-303.html)) [VERIFY: abstract only].

**[Inference]** The notes derive the consequence for daily profiles. When demand d(t) exceeds the discharge rate μ = C(1 − δ), counts at or below the bottleneck show a flat plateau at μ. The plateau is **lower** than the demand peak, **wider** (it lasts until the queue clears, after the demand peak ends) and **later-centred**. The capacity drop δ widens it further and also makes the plateau understate pre-queue capacity. A profile fitted to such counts is unbiased on light days but **compresses the upper tail** of the day-to-day demand distribution. The error is therefore largest exactly where control matters. Total daily volume is conserved if the window brackets the episode, so heavy and normal days can look alike in bottleneck daily totals. TOD switching times set from count profiles switch late in both directions. A plant calibrated on counts, with no drop, never reproduces the observed queue and removes the headroom the controller is meant to recover. The notes found **no documented field case** where a freeway control plan was shown to fail specifically because counts hid demand.

Five families of methods recover unconstrained demand. They are: counting upstream of the maximum queue extent; throughput plus queue accumulation (N-curves, "demand = capacity + queue"); shockwave back-calculation from the speed of the queue tail; volume–delay-function inversion; and model-based Kalman or dynamic-OD estimation. The only verified accuracy figures are *arterial*. Using INRIX probe speeds, the shockwave and VDF methods gave **MAPE 2.5 % and 2.8 %** in simulation (individual errors about −12 % to +6 %) and "**less than 4 %** error" against video counts on Indian River Road, Virginia Beach ([Cetin et al., 2021](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/60865/dot_60865_DS1.pdf)) [read in part]. The shockwave method transfers to freeways as generic kinematic-wave physics; the VDF variant uses a signal-delay equation and does not. Freeway-specific work is [SAGE] or abstract-level. It includes Huntsinger and Rouphail's "demand = capacity + queue" VDF calibration, known only from Cetin et al.'s summary ([Huntsinger and Rouphail, 2011](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/60865/dot_60865_DS1.pdf)) [SAGE] [VERIFY: not read]; Pan et al.'s Greenshields-grounded "symmetric method" ([Pan et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/03611981251394673)) [SAGE] [VERIFY: snippet only]; and point-detector-plus-AVI queue-length estimation ([Fakharian Qom et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.3141/2616-03)) [SAGE] [VERIFY: snippet only]. Model-based OD estimation is biased under congestion unless the loading model represents queueing and spill-back ([Frederix, Viti and Tampère, via ORBilu](https://orbilu.uni.lu/bitstream/10993/7405/1/hierarchical.pdf)) [VERIFY: snippet only; exact paper unconfirmed]. A Minnesota OD-split study was tested only in a simulation where "no congestion was observed" ([Wu, Davis and Levinson, 2004](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/39368/dot_39368_DS1.pdf)) [VERIFY: abstract and selected passages]. **[Inference]** For heavy-day profiles, prefer upstream counts beyond the maximum queue plus ramp queue-entrance counts. Otherwise use N-curve reconstruction, with shockwave back-calculation as a cheap cross-check. Methods disagree most at onset and at the peak minute.

## Learned anticipation gains shrink to 0–4 % against tuned feedback

None of the RL freeway-control papers read in full feeds an explicit time-of-day, day-type or demand-forecast feature, and none tests on held-out *real* days. Variability comes from noise around one nominal profile. Hua and Ma use OD demand with a standard deviation of 30 % of the mean, and a 60–150 % scaled stress test of the same pattern ([Hua and Ma, 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.03395)) [read: relevant sections]. Airaldi et al. generate random two-peak profiles per episode and warn that "a performance drop is anticipated" for unforeseen shapes ([Airaldi, De Schutter and Dabiri, 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.08820)) [read: relevant sections]. **[Inference]** With no day-type latent in training, a policy cannot learn day-type-conditional anticipation, and no paper ablates the history input (e.g. Hua and Ma's h_t). Zhou et al. present learned anticipation folded into a policy "in pure feedback form" as an alternative to explicit predictors ([Zhou et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1155/2020/8813467)) [VERIFY: abstract only].

Forecast quality is the hinge for MPC. In the AMOC case, imperfect demand prediction costs about 16–29 % TTS relative to perfect foresight. **[Inference]** That loss is comparable in absolute size to the remaining MPC-vs-ALINEA gap, and shorter re-optimisation intervals recover part of it. A scenario-based min-max MPC under demand uncertainty "performs best", while adding uncertainty handling to PI-ALINEA-type feedback gives "minor" improvements ([Liu et al., 2016](https://www.dcsc.tudelft.nl/~bdeschutter/pub/abs/15_045.html)) [VERIFY: abstract only; no numbers]. Bellemans et al. found MPC "not very sensitive to the length of the prediction horizon", and two-ramp coordination added nothing because both ramps hit queue limits together. Hegyi et al. bound flow-improving control at about the capacity-drop margin ("approximately 5–10 %"). They cite a claim that "an increase of outflow of 5 % may result in an decrease of the total time spent in the network of 20 %" [sic] ([Hegyi, De Schutter and Hellendoorn, 2005](https://www.bartdeschutter.org/publications/03-016-model-predictive-control-optimal/03-016-model-predictive-control-optimal.pdf)) [Papageorgiou et al. 1998 itself VERIFY: not read].

**[Inference]** In this literature "anticipation" is confounded with coordination and with exact model knowledge. No study ablates pre-emptive metering before the peak with dispersion. Where a strong reactive coordinated controller (HERO) is used, the reported gap to proactive optimal control is small, though only at abstract level. The gains that survive a tuned feedback comparator are about 0–4 % median, with wide dispersion. The notes call this consistent with the repo prior of 1.5–6 % hybrid gains and v6's 0–5 %, and it is a synthesis of 2–3 papers, not a meta-analytic estimate. The large 30–70 % headlines are almost always against no control, or against untuned, individually tuned or queue-hobbled feedback. No paper combines held-out real days, pre-registered metrics and a jointly tuned coordinated baseline.

## Open data with daily patterns exists; ramp demand is the weak link

Two open sources stand out for multi-year, per-lane, sub-5-minute freeway data in a ramp-metering context. **MnDOT RTMC (Twin Cities)** provides 30-s volume, occupancy and speed from more than 4,500 loop detectors, packaged as daily zip archives ([Kwon, UMN Duluth archive page](https://www.d.umn.edu/~tkwon/TMCdata/TMCarchive.html)) [VERIFY]. A public JSON feed pattern for 30-s files is documented ([Metropolitan Council tc.sensors README](https://github.com/Metropolitan-Council/tc.sensors/blob/main/README.Rmd)) [VERIFY]. **Caltrans PeMS** remained online on 2026-10-04 behind a free, manually approved account, with "over 39,000 individual detectors", mainline, on-ramp and off-ramp stations, and a decade-plus archive ([Caltrans PeMS](https://pems.dot.ca.gov/)). The Caltrans-funded open-source modernisation by Compiler LLC "has ended and the repository is now archived" (October 2025), and nothing seen says whether it changes the legacy site ([Compiler PeMS docs](https://docs.compiler.la/pems/)). About 30 % of PeMS roadside data is reported as questionable ([Caltrans research note](https://dot.ca.gov/-/media/dot-media/programs/research-innovation-system-information/documents/research-notes/task3861-rns-11-21-a11y.pdf)) [VERIFY: snippet attribution uncertain]. These access statuses are as seen on 2026-10-04 and may change. Other sources include NDW (Netherlands): open minute data from >100,000 locations, with history given as "from 2010" or "back to July 2011" on different pages, a conflict ([NDW Dexter](https://english.ndw.nu/products-and-services/dexter)) [VERIFY]. UK WebTRIS is a key-free JSON API under a "Free Other (Open)" licence ([DfT Find Transport Data](https://findtransportdata.dft.gov.uk/dataset/webtris-traffic-flow-api)). Germany's public motorway counts are mainly hourly ([BMV](https://www.bmv.de/SharedDocs/DE/Artikel/StB/strassenverkehrszaehlung-2025.html)) [VERIFY], and WSDOT's I-5 Seattle 20-s set covers only May–October 2011 ([data.gov](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/seattle-20-second-freeway)). Trajectory sets are far too short for daily patterns: highD averages about 17 minutes per recording and NGSIM 45 minutes per highway ([Krajewski et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.05642)) [VERIFY]. I-24 MOTION's first release has at least 4 h of trajectories for each of 10 days ([Gloudemans et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.11198v1)) [VERIFY]. ML benchmarks (METR-LA, PEMS-BAY, PeMSD4/8, LargeST) are mainline-only PeMS or LA subsets covering a few months ([Li et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.01926); [Guo et al., 2019](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/3881/3759); [Liu et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.08259)) [all VERIFY].

The notes record several ways others have used these data. Guardiola et al. used MnDOT I-94 1-minute data for functional day-type monitoring (above). Srivastava and Geroliminis used Twin Cities ramp-metered merges to show that **"the level of drop depends on the ratio of mainline vs. ramp flow"**, and embedded the drop in a two-capacity Godunov CTM with a memory rule ([Srivastava and Geroliminis, 2013](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/188341)) [VERIFY]. Chan, Raghavan and Wu calibrate METANET on PeMS with open code. They estimate ramp flows *jointly* from mainline data because ramp detectors are "frequently unavailable or unreliable" ([Chan, Raghavan and Wu, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23042)) [VERIFY: abstract only]. The Alicante–Murcia SUMO scenario was calibrated over **9 full days on 97 km** with Spanish road-authority flows, using "Clone Feedback" demand calibration (MIT licence) ([González-Delicado et al., 2021](https://github.com/msepulcre/Alicante-Murcia-SUMO-Scenario)) [abstract read]. An I-24 SUMO calibration fitted IDM and LC2013 parameters to 5-minute data, with flow RMSE 305 veh/h/lane ([Wang, de Souza and Karbowski, 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.19090)) [VERIFY: tool summary]. A MARL VSL trained in simulation was deployed on 17 miles of I-24 with 67 controllers ([Zhang et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08021)) [abstract read].

**[Inference]** The notes rank the sources for daily profiles with ramp metering as MnDOT > PeMS > NDW > WebTRIS. Imputed PeMS values smooth out the breakdown dynamics a VSL or ramp-metering study needs, so a pre-registered "% observed" filter is necessary. Observed on-ramp passage flows under active metering equal the metered release, not demand. Day types should therefore be clustered on *upstream boundary demand* (mainline in plus ramp queue and passage), not on bottleneck throughput. Jointly estimated ramp flows make demand a model output, which is circular if the same model then evaluates control. The notes' extraction recipe (site screen, quality filter, speed-drop detection of recurrent active bottlenecks, oblique-curve drop measurement, demand clustering, assignment of clusters to seed blocks before fitting) is their own synthesis, not a published protocol.

## No published SUMO plant yet passes both the discharge and the drop gate

The notes found **no published SUMO study that calibrates a freeway merge or lane drop to an empirical capacity drop and an empirical queue-discharge rate at the same time**. A benchmark of three microsimulators reports a capacity drop "in SymuVia and Aimsun but not in SUMO", with Aimsun's maximum about **17 %** and SymuVia's **7 %**, against field observations of 5–30 % ([Boukhellouf, Buisson and Chiabaut, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12544-023-00586-1)) [VERIFY: snippet only; SUMO version and parameters unknown]. A 2025 ANNSIM snippet says Krauss "overestimates the bottleneck capacity by **46 %**" at a SUMO lane drop ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.22360/ANNSIM.2025.ANSS.43)) [VERIFY: snippet; authors unconfirmed]. SUMO maintainers say realism must come from stochasticity (Krauss `sigma` or EIDM rather than deterministic IDM), driver heterogeneity and calibrated `lcCooperative`/`lcAssertive`. They note that the default lane-change model is LC2013 ([GitHub #16198](https://github.com/eclipse-sumo/sumo/issues/16198); [GitHub #16659](https://github.com/eclipse-sumo/sumo/issues/16659)). The docs suggest lengthening acceleration lanes to offset cautious default merging ([SUMO docs: Motorways](https://sumo.dlr.de/docs/Simulation/Motorways.html)) [VERIFY wording]. A 2026 SUMO-conference recipe recommends ≥ 5 driver classes, assertive ramp drivers, unregulated merges and a ban on mainline use of the auxiliary lane. It reports no realism validation, and its mainline vTypes use `tau` = 10.0–8.5 s ([Riehl, Kouvelas and Makridis, 2026](https://sumo.dlr.de/preprints/pre-print-3284.pdf)) [read: §3.1 and route file]. **[Inference]** With Krauss, that `tau` would cap mainline flow near 350 veh/h/lane. The notes cannot confirm whether this is intended, so the recipe should not be copied as validated.

Commercial tools needed model surgery to pass. Wang et al. say faithful reproduction of drop at merges in AIMSUN and VISSIM "is not yet easy", because default ramp vehicles wait too long on the acceleration lane. After replacing Gipps with IDM and adding a forced-merge model calibrated on the Dutch A20, they obtained a first-merge capacity of **about 2,085 veh/h/lane**, an outflow "dropped by **14.5 %**" after about 15 minutes, reverse-lambda fundamental diagrams, and drop prevention by ALINEA ([Wang et al., 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/15/3/2050)) [§1–4 largely read]. Their METANET case lists 2,050 veh/h/lane with a 15 % drop rate (table parse VERIFY), and their Shanghai field merge shows an 11.9 % drop. An Aimsun MSc model obtained a **5.52 %** drop, rising towards 100 % as merge distance shrank ([Chong, 2017](https://escholarship.org/content/qt18d9s080/qt18d9s080.pdf)) [partly read]. Chong also attributes 3 % to Banks (2010), 8–10 % to Cassidy and Bertini, and 18 % to Chung et al. (2007), so on the notes' reading the gate's "3–18 %" range spans several sources rather than one (Banks 2010 is known only through Chong). VISSIM 10 and MOTUS, even calibrated, "are as yet unable to simulate turbulent traffic around motorway ramps realistically" ([van Beinum et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1061/jtepbs.0000296)) [VERIFY: abstract]. A calibration benchmark on SUMO I-24 scenarios finds "all methods are insufficient to capture stop-and-go behavior", with waves degrading "to non-existence" at scale ([Hickert et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.19642)) [partly read]. The notes contain no literature source for the 1,600 veh/h/lane discharge floor itself; it is the repo's gate value, to be checked against field fits such as Knoop and Daamen's separate estimation of free-flow capacity and queue discharge ([Knoop and Daamen, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/21680566.2016.1256239)) [VERIFY: abstract].

Macroscopic plants pass a drop gate *by construction*. First-order LWR/CTM models cannot reproduce the drop without extension ([Kontorinaki et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trb.2017.10.014)) [VERIFY: abstract snippet], while METANET yields it "to an extent" (Wang et al., 2023). Cao, Nilsson and Coogan show that a controller designed while ignoring the drop "may be significantly suboptimal" in a drop-aware CTM ([Cao, Nilsson and Coogan, 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.10347)) [abstract/introduction read]. METANET has real-site validations and field-controller lineage: Amsterdam, Paris and Monash HERO ([Kotsialos et al., 2002](https://doi.org/10.1109/tits.2002.806804)) [VERIFY]; ([Papamichail et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.3141/2178-02)) [SAGE] [VERIFY; an open EPFL copy is listed]. SUMO's Eissfeldt mesoscopic model "generally underestimate[s]" congestion and does not fully follow LWR ([Ni et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.09282)) [abstract read].

Project-internal context recorded in the notes (not literature): the repo's signalised plant passed with Krauss at 1,854 veh/h/lane, while the freeway variants failed at 900–1,528 veh/h/lane. **[Inference]** That pattern points to lateral and merge artefacts rather than car-following. The notes propose a single-lane jam-release test as the cheapest discriminating check. They rank options as: a two-capacity CTM or METANET with a field-fitted drop swept over 0/5/10/15 % for exploration and DRL training; a rebuilt SUMO merge (Krauss/EIDM with `sigma`, ≥ 5 vTypes, assertive and impatient ramp drivers, long acceleration lanes, auxiliary-lane ban) for confirmation; and Alicante–Murcia for full-day external validity, with undocumented bottleneck realism. Both gate criteria should be measured at the same downstream mainline detector, because the drop "could only be seen precisely at activated bottleneck locations" (Wang et al., 2023).

## What this implies for a controller that learns daily demand patterns

**[Inference, this report]** Taken together, the five notes bound the headroom from three sides. First, *information*: day types are about 3–6 calendar-aligned clusters. A non-learning lookup table (calendar → parameters, tuned on tuning seeds) already holds most of the signal, and it is the natural template for the repo's comparator I, mirroring signal-practice traffic-responsive plan selection. The residual signal sits in outlier days, which the only quantified source mis-types about a quarter of the time at 08:00. Second, *control authority*: tuned feedback regulates to a capacity or critical-occupancy set-point that is largely independent of demand. The notes argue that day-type knowledge therefore mostly informs *whether* and *how hard* to act (activation windows, queue limits, aggressiveness), not the trajectory feedback already tracks. The evidence that survives tuned comparators is 0–4 % median with wide spread, which sits below the repo's 10 % gate. Third, *plant physics*: the headroom that does exist concentrates on heavy days (Riyadh: metering mattered only at 110 % demand) and scales with the capacity drop. Both are exactly what count-calibrated demand and default SUMO merges suppress, and what an assumed-drop CTM inflates.

The protocol consequences follow directly, and they match roadmap §0/§9. Demand libraries should be built from upstream boundary demand, not bottleneck counts, and should keep the heavy-day tail. Held-out *days*, not just seeds, should be drawn from a day-type library. Comparators should include a tuned ALINEA/PI-ALINEA, HERO where coordination exists, a calendar lookup table, and an MPC whose forecast is fitted on separate days. Every gain should be reported against a swept capacity-drop magnitude, and an ablation should remove the time-of-day or day-type inputs. If a learned layer still clears 10 % under those conditions, that would be informative because nothing in the notes predicts it. A KILL would be consistent with every quantified source in the notes.

## Conclusion

The notes shift the question from "can DRL learn daily patterns?" to "is there anything left to learn once the calendar and a tuned feedback loop have acted?". On present evidence the answer is: little, except on heavy and atypical days. Those days are the ones that count-based calibration hides, that default SUMO merges fail to break down on, and that early-morning classifiers mis-type. The value of a daily-pattern learner is therefore inseparable from plant realism and demand reconstruction. The thesis's headroom gate is effectively a joint test of all three, which makes the plant gates (T1) and upstream-demand reconstruction the cheapest places to secure, or kill, the claim.

A second, less obvious implication concerns the literature's large gains. They come almost entirely from weak comparators (no control, smoothness-tuned or queue-hobbled ALINEA) or from foreknowledge (exact demand, plant = model). A comparator that is honestly tuned and forecast on separate days is therefore not a formality: it is the main determinant of the reported effect. **[Inference, this report]** A carefully executed negative result against such comparators would at least be a defensible, reportable outcome under the repo's no-weak-baselines rule, although it would not by itself meet the thesis aim of showing DRL improving on rule-based or predictive control.
